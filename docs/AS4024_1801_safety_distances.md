# AS/NZS 4024.1801:2014 — Safety Distances to Prevent Danger Zones Being Reached by Upper and Lower Limbs

## Correspondence

This standard is identical to ISO 13857:2008. All clause numbers, table numbers, and figure numbers correspond directly to the ISO original.

> **Supersession note:** AS/NZS 4024.1802 (Safety distances — lower limbs, 2006 edition) was withdrawn. Its content is fully absorbed into this standard (1801:2014). There is no separate current 1802 standard.

---

## 1 Scope

This standard establishes values for safety distances in both industrial and non-industrial environments to prevent machinery danger zones from being reached by upper and lower limbs. The safety distances apply to protective structures and are intended for situations where adequate risk reduction can be achieved by distance alone. It also gives information about distances to impede free access by the lower limbs (see Annex B).

This standard covers persons of 14 years and older (the 5th percentile stature of 14 year olds is approximately 1 400 mm). In addition, for upper limbs only, it provides information for children older than 3 years (5th percentile stature of 3 year olds is approximately 900 mm) where reaching through openings needs to be addressed.

> **NOTE 1:** Data for preventing lower limb access for children is not considered.

> **NOTE 2:** These safety distances will not provide sufficient protection against certain hazards, for example, radiation and emission of substances. For such hazards, additional or other measures need to be used.

> **NOTE 3:** This standard is not intended to provide measures against reaching a hazard zone by climbing over. Where climbing over is foreseeable, supplementary protective measures must be applied.

> **NOTE 4:** Because safety distances depend on size, there will be some people of extreme dimensions who will be able to reach hazard zones even when the requirements of this standard are complied with.

## 3 Terms and Definitions

### 3.1 Protective Structure
A safeguard (e.g. a guard, an impeding device) or other physical obstruction (e.g. a part of a machine) which restricts the movement of the body and/or a part of it in order to prevent reaching hazard zones.

### 3.2 Safety Distance (safe separation distance) — sr
Minimum distance a protective structure is required to be placed from a hazard zone.

## 4 Safety Distances to Prevent Access by Upper and Lower Limbs

### 4.1 General

#### 4.1.1 Assumptions

The safety distances in this standard have been derived by making the following assumptions:

- The protective structures and any openings in them retain their shape and position
- Safety distances are measured from the surface restricting the body or the relevant part of the body
- Persons may force parts of the body over protective structures or through openings in an attempt to reach the hazard zone
- The reference plane is a level at which persons would normally stand, but is not necessarily the floor (e.g. a working platform could be the reference plane)
- There is some contact with the reference plane while wearing shoes (use of high-soled shoes, climbing and jumping are not included)
- No aids such as chairs or ladders are used to change the reference plane
- No aids such as rods or tools are used to extend the natural reach of the upper limbs

#### 4.1.2 Risk Assessment

Before determining the safety distance that prevents people from reaching hazard zones, it is necessary to decide whether to use values for **high risk** or **low risk**. A risk assessment (see ISO 12100-1 and ISO 14121-1) shall be conducted. The risk assessment shall be based on the probability of occurrence of an injury and the foreseeable severity of that injury.

- Where there is a **low risk**, at least the values in **Table 1** shall be used (see 4.2.2.1.1)
- Where the risk is **not low**, **Table 2**, the high risk table, shall be used (see 4.2.2.1.2)

Where several tables apply, the most restrictive values shall be taken.

> **NOTE:** Only risks arising from hazards such as friction or abrasion, where long-term or irreversible damage to the body is not foreseeable, can lead to low risks. In most machinery safety contexts, Table 2 (high risk) applies.

Where a hazard zone is positioned above the reference plane, the following minimum heights apply:

|---|---|
| Low risk | h ≥ 2 500 mm |
| High risk | h ≥ 2 700 mm |

Where the hazard zone height meets these values, no additional horizontal safety distance is required for the upward reach scenario.

##### 4.2.4.3 Openings of Irregular Shape

In the case of irregular openings, the following steps shall be carried out in order:

**a)** Determine:
- the diameter of the smallest round opening
- the side of the smallest square opening, and
- the width of the narrowest slot opening

into which the irregular opening can be completely inserted.

**b)** Select the corresponding three distances according to either Table 4 or Table 5.

**c)** The shortest safety distance of the three values selected in b) may be used.

## Annex A (Informative) — Use of Tables 1 and 2 with Intermediate Values

### A.1 Principle

The following examples explain the use of Tables 1 and 2 when values other than those given in the tables themselves will be used. When known values of a, b, or c lie between two table values, select the table value that gives the **more conservative (safer) result**:

- For **a** (hazard zone height): use the row with the **higher** a value (takes the more conservative row)
- For **b** (protective structure height): use the column with the **lower** b value (takes the more conservative column)
- For **c** (safety distance): use the row with the **greater** c value

### A.2 Example 1 — Finding b with known a and c

If the height of the hazard zone is 1 500 mm and the horizontal safety distance from the proposed protective structure is 800 mm, determine the required height b of the protective structure using Table 2 (high risk):

1. In Table 2, find the row where a = 1 500 mm. Because 1 500 mm is not in the table, select a = 1 400 mm (next higher row that is safer — gives values greater than for a = 1 500 mm).
2. In that row, find the column where c ≥ 800 mm. The value c = 800 is found at b = 1 800 mm.
3. Therefore b = 1 800 mm.

> 📐 See AS/NZS 4024.1801:2014 Figure A.1 — Example 1 diagram showing the selected row and column highlighted in Table 2

### A.3 Example 2 — Finding c with known a and b

If b = 1 300 mm and a = 2 300 mm, determine c using Table 2:

1. b = 1 300 mm is not in the table. Select b = 1 200 mm (next lower value — more conservative).
2. a = 2 300 mm is not in the table. Select a = 2 400 mm (next higher value — more conservative).
3. At row a = 2 400, column b = 1 200: c = 1 200 mm (from Footnote b: b < 1 400 mm should not be used without additional safety measures).

> 📐 See AS/NZS 4024.1801:2014 Figure A.2 — Example 2 diagram

### A.4 Example 3 — Finding a with known b and c

If b = 1 700 mm and c = 850 mm, determine the admissible range of hazard zone heights a using Table 2:

1. b = 1 700 mm → select b = 1 600 mm column (more conservative).
2. In that column, find rows where c ≥ 850 mm → c = 900 mm is the next greater value. Multiple rows satisfy this condition.
3. The resulting range of a values where the arrangement is **not** protected tells the designer which heights the protective structure does not cover.

> 📐 See AS/NZS 4024.1801:2014 Figure A.3 — Example 3 diagram showing unprotected hazard zone height range highlighted

### A.5 Example 4 — Grid openings

For a high risk; if a = 1 800 mm and the protective structure is a grid of height b = 2 000 mm with square openings (50 mm × 50 mm), two checks apply:

- **Reaching over (Table 2):** a = 1 800, b = 2 000 → c = 600 mm
- **Reaching through square opening (Table 4):** e = 50 mm → sr = 850 mm

The larger value governs: **c = 850 mm** shall be used.

## Annex B (Informative) — Distances to Impede Free Access by Lower Limbs

### B.1 Purpose

An additional protective structure can be used to restrict the free movement of the lower limbs under existing protective structures. The distances in this annex relate to the height h from the ground or reference plane to the protective structure and a horizontal distance l from the person to the protective structure.

> **NOTE:** These distances are not safety distances and additional precautions could be required to restrict access.

This method provides limited protection; in many cases other methods will be more appropriate. Where there is a risk of slipping or misuse, applying the values in Table B.1 can be inappropriate. There shall be no interpolation in Table B.1.

> 📐 See AS/NZS 4024.1801:2014 Figure B.1 — Impeding free movement under protective structures, showing Cases 1, 2, and 3 with h (height to protective structure) and l (horizontal distance) labelled

### B.2 Cases

Three cases are defined based on the geometry of the protective structure relative to the person (see Figure B.1):

- **Case 1:** Person outside the structure, single structure
- **Case 2:** Person between two parallel structures
- **Case 3:** Person at a corner between two perpendicular structures

### B.3 Table B.1 — Distances Where Access of the Lower Limbs Is Restricted

All dimensions in **mm**. h = height up to protective structure; l = distance for impedance.

| Height **h** up to protective structure (mm) | Case 1 — Distance **l** | Case 2 — Distance **l** | Case 3 — Distance **l** |
|---|---|---|---|
| h ≤ 200 | ≥ 340 | ≥ 665 | ≥ 290 |
| 200 < h ≤ 400 | ≥ 550 | ≥ 765 | ≥ 615 |
| 400 < h ≤ 600 | ≥ 850 | ≥ 950 | ≥ 800 |
| 600 < h ≤ 800 | ≥ 950 | ≥ 950 | ≥ 900 |
| 800 < h ≤ 1 000 | ≥ 1 125 | ≥ 1 195 | ≥ 1 015 |

> **NOTE:** Slot openings with e > 180 mm and square and round openings with e > 240 mm will allow access for the whole body.
