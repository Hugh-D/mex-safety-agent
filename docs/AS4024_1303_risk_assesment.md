# Safety of Machinery — Part 1303
## Risk Assessment: Practical Guidance and Examples of Methods
*Based on ISO/TR 12100-1303*

---

## 1. Scope

This Technical Report provides practical guidance on conducting risk assessment for machinery in accordance with ISO 12100. It describes methods and tools for each step in the process and gives examples of different measures to reduce risk.

Intended users: designers, technicians, and safety specialists involved in the design, installation, or modification of machinery.

Annex A provides a specific example of a risk assessment and risk reduction process.

- **ISO 12100:2010** — *Safety of Machinery: General Principles for Design — Risk Assessment and Risk Reduction*

Terms and definitions from ISO 12100 apply, plus:

**3.1 Manufacturer / Supplier**
Entity (e.g. designer, manufacturer, contractor, installer, integrator) who provides equipment or services associated with machinery or parts of machinery.

## 4. Preparation for Risk Assessment

### 4.1 General

Define the objectives and scope at the outset. The risk assessment covers the whole machinery, including the control system, and should be carried out by the manufacturer.

### 4.2 Team Approach

#### 4.2.1 General

Risk assessment is generally more thorough and effective when performed by a team. Team size varies according to:

- the risk assessment approach selected
- the complexity of the machine
- the process in which the machine is utilized

A dedicated team leader should be clearly identified — the success of the assessment depends on their skills.

#### 4.2.2 Composition and Role of Team Members

The team leader is fully responsible for planning, performing, and documenting the assessment. Team members should:

- answer technical questions about design and function
- have actual operating, set-up, and maintenance experience
- know the accident history of this machine type
- understand relevant regulations and standards, particularly ISO 12100
- understand human factors (ISO 12100:2010, 5.5.3.4)

#### 4.2.3 Selection of Methods and Tools

When selecting methods, consider:

- the complexity and potential for harm of the machine
- the likely nature of hazards
- the purpose of the risk assessment
- the skills, experience, and preferences of the team

See Clause 5 for additional guidance on method selection.

#### 4.2.4 Source of Information for Risk Assessment

Information required is listed in ISO 12100:2010, 5.2. Sources include: technical drawings, diagrams, photos, video footage, information for use, maintenance information, and SOPs. Access to similar machinery or a prototype is often useful.

## 5. Risk Assessment Process

### 5.1 General

The following subclauses explain what must be considered at each step of the risk assessment process (see ISO 12100:2010, Figure 1).

### 5.2 Determination of the Limits of the Machinery

#### 5.2.1 General

Objective: produce a clear description of the mechanical and physical properties, functional capabilities, intended use, reasonably foreseeable misuse, and the environment in which the machine will be used and maintained.

#### 5.2.2 Functions of the Machinery (Machine-Based)

Machinery can be described in terms of distinct parts, mechanisms or functions:

- Power supply
- Control
- Modes of operation
- Feeding
- Movement/travelling
- Lifting
- Machine frame or chassis (stability/mobility)
- Attachments

Every mode of operation and all phases of use must be considered, including human-machine interaction.

#### 5.2.3 Uses of the Machinery (Task-Based)

For all persons intended to interact with the machine, describe use in terms of the tasks associated with intended use and reasonably foreseeable misuse.

Consider:
- Information for use supplied with the machinery
- The easiest or quickest way to carry out a task (may differ from manuals)
- Reflex behaviour during malfunction or failure
- Human error

### 5.3 Hazard Identification

#### 5.3.1 General

Objective: produce a list of hazards, hazardous situations, and/or hazardous events describing how and when a hazardous situation can lead to harm.

**Hazard identification is the most important step in any risk assessment.** Unidentified hazards cannot be reduced. The process must be as systematic and comprehensive as practicable.

Useful starting reference: ISO 12100:2010, Annex B (generic checklist).

#### 5.3.2 Methods for Hazard Identification

Two general approaches (see Figure 1 — Top-down and Bottom-up):

**Top-down approach**
Starts from a checklist of potential consequences (e.g. cutting, crushing, hearing loss) and works back to identify hazards. Drawback: relies on the completeness of the checklist; inexperienced teams may miss hazards not listed.

**Bottom-up approach**
Starts by examining all hazards and considering all possible ways something can go wrong. More comprehensive but can be prohibitively time-consuming.

> Checklists should not be interpreted as exhaustive — they should encourage creative thinking beyond the list.

#### 5.3.3 Recording of Information

Hazard identification records must clearly describe:

- The hazard and its location (hazard zone)
- The hazardous situation — types of people exposed (operators, maintenance, passers-by) and tasks that expose them
- How the hazardous situation leads to harm via a hazardous event or prolonged exposure
- Nature and severity of harm in machine-specific terms (e.g. "fingers crushed by down-stroke of press when adjusting work-piece")
- Existing protective/risk reduction measures and their effectiveness

#### 5.3.4 Example Tool: Hazard Identification by Forms (ISO 12100:2010, Annex B)

**Three steps:**
1. Determine the extent of the system: phases of the machine life cycle, parts, and/or functions.
2. Define tasks performed by people interacting with or near the machine for each selected phase (use ISO 12100:2010, Table B.3).
3. Examine each task/operation in each hazard zone for relevant hazards and hazardous situations — using either top-down or bottom-up approach (reference ISO 12100:2010 Tables B.1, B.3, B.4).

**Documentation:** Use blank form (Table A.3) to record hazard identification results.

### 5.4 Risk Estimation

#### 5.4.1 General

Risk has two main elements:
- **Severity of harm** (5.4.2)
- **Probability of occurrence of that harm** (5.4.3)

Risk is generally expressed as a level, index, or score, and can also be descriptive.

#### 5.4.2 Severity of Harm

Estimate the risk of a range of representative severities; use the most severe harm that can realistically occur (worst credible).

> The most severe harm is not always the most probable — using either extreme alone can lead to inappropriate estimation. Estimate a range and use the one that gives the highest risk.

Note: Lower energy hazards generally correlate with lower severity. Severity also relates to the body part exposed (e.g. crushing hazards are generally fatal if the whole body or head is exposed).

#### 5.4.3 Probability of Occurrence of Harm

##### 5.4.3.1 General

Estimate probability by considering:

- Exposure of person(s) to the hazard *(ISO 12100:2010, 5.5.2.3.1)*
- Probability of occurrence of a hazardous event *(5.5.2.3.2)*
- Technical and human possibilities to avoid or limit the harm *(5.5.2.3.3)*

##### 5.4.3.2 Probability of Cumulative Harm (Health Aspects)

Hazardous situations leading to cumulative harm (e.g. dermatitis, occupational asthma, hearing loss, repetitive strain injury) are handled differently from acute harm.

- A chronic health condition (e.g. hearing loss) depends on **total dose over time** — made up of exposures of different durations and levels from several sources.
- Example: For respiratory harm, damage depends on dose and/or concentration. For hearing loss, it depends on noise levels, duration, and repetitiveness.

## 6. Risk Estimation Tools

### 6.1 General

Three main tool types:

- **Risk matrix** (6.2)
- **Risk graph** (6.3)
- **Numerical scoring** (6.4)
- **Hybrid tools** combining methods (6.5)

> The choice of tool is less important than the discipline of the process. Resources are better directed at risk reduction efforts than at achieving absolute precision in risk estimation.

Any risk estimation tool must address at minimum: **severity of harm** and **probability of occurrence of harm**.

---

### 6.2 Risk Matrix

#### 6.2.1 General

A multidimensional table combining any class of severity of harm with any class of probability of occurrence. Most common matrices are two-dimensional but can have up to four dimensions.

Cell intersections give the estimated risk level — expressed as an index (e.g. 1–6 or A–D) or qualitative term ("low", "medium", "high").

Number of cells can vary (e.g. 4 to 36). Too few cells may not provide sufficient information on whether protective measures provide adequate risk reduction. Too many cells can make the matrix confusing.

#### 6.2.2 Example Risk Matrix (Table 1)

| Probability of Occurrence | Catastrophic | Serious | Moderate | Minor |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Very likely | **High** | **High** | **High** | Medium |
| Likely | **High** | **High** | Medium | Low |
| Unlikely | Medium | Medium | Low | Negligible |
| Remote | Low | Low | Negligible | Negligible |

**Severity levels:**
- **Catastrophic** — death or permanent disabling injury/illness (unable to return to work)
- **Serious** — severe debilitating injury/illness (able to return to work at some point)
- **Moderate** — significant injury/illness requiring more than first aid (able to return to same job)
- **Minor** — no injury or slight injury requiring no more than first aid (little or no lost work time)

**Probability levels:**
- **Very likely** — near certain to occur
- **Likely** — can occur
- **Unlikely** — not likely to occur
- **Remote** — so unlikely as to be near zero

**Factors affecting probability of occurrence of harm:**
- Frequency and duration of exposure
- Number of persons exposed
- Personnel who perform tasks
- Machine/task history
- Workplace environment
- Human factors
- Reliability of safety functions
- Possibility to defeat or circumvent protective measures
- Ability to maintain protective measures
- Ability to avoid harm

#### 6.2.3 Deriving Risk Level

Once severity and probability are estimated, the risk matrix maps these to a risk level (see Table 1). Risk levels will typically yield an array of low to high risks. Since risk estimation is usually subjective, risk levels will also be subjective.

---

### 6.3 Risk Graph

#### 6.3.1 General

A risk graph is based on a decision tree. Each node represents a parameter of risk (severity, exposure, probability of a hazardous event, possibility of avoidance). Each branch represents a class of that parameter.

For each hazardous situation, a class is allocated to each parameter. The path through the graph produces a risk index or level (e.g. 'high', 'medium', 'low', 1–6, or A–F).

> Risk graphs are useful for illustrating the amount of risk reduction provided by a protective measure. They can become cumbersome if there are more than two branches for more than one parameter — hybrid methods are preferred in those cases.

#### 6.3.2 Example Risk Graph Parameters (Figure 3)

**Severity (S):**
- **S1** — slight injury (usually reversible: scratch, laceration, bruise, light wound requiring first aid, etc.) — no more than two days incapable of performing the same task
- **S2** — serious injury (usually irreversible, including fatality: broken or torn-out/crushed limb, fracture, serious injury requiring stitches, major MST etc.) — more than two days incapable of performing the same task

**Frequency and/or Duration of Exposure (F):**
- **F1** — seldom to quite often and/or short duration (twice or less per work shift or less than 15 min cumulated exposure per work shift)
- **F2** — frequent to continuous and/or long duration (more than twice per work shift or more than 15 min cumulated exposure per work shift)

**Probability of Occurrence of a Hazardous Event (O):**
- **O1** — low (so unlikely occurrence may not be experienced); mature technology, proven in safety application
- **O2** — medium (likely to occur sometime); technical failure in the last two years; inappropriate human action by well-trained person with more than six months' experience
- **O3** — high (likely to occur frequently); technical failure regularly observed (every six months or less); inappropriate human action by untrained person with less than six months' experience

**Possibility of Avoidance or Reduction of Harm (A):**
- **A1** — possible under some conditions: parts move at less than 0.25 m/s⁻¹ and the worker is familiar with the risk and with the indication of a hazardous situation; depends on particular conditions (temperature, noise, ergonomics)
- **A2** — impossible

**Risk Index:** 1 (lowest) to 6 (highest)
- RI 1–2 = lowest risk
- RI 3–4 = medium risk
- RI 5–6 = highest risk

> **Note:** This risk graph is most appropriate for acute hazards (mechanical, electrical, thermal). For health hazards (noise, ergonomics), compare results with specific dedicated tools.

---

### 6.4 Numerical Scoring

#### 6.4.1 General

Numerical scoring assigns numerical values (scores) to classes of each parameter. Scores are combined (e.g. addition or multiplication) to produce a numerical risk score. Results are often then grouped into qualitative classifications (high, medium, low).

Scoring can give an impression of objectivity, but the allocation of scores remains subjective. The ability to select one number from within an integer range allows for more refined choices than qualitative terms — but can give a false impression of numerical accuracy.

#### 6.4.2 Example Numerical Scoring Tool

**Severity Score (SS):**
- Catastrophic: SS = 100
- Serious: 99 ≥ SS ≥ 90
- Moderate: 89 ≥ SS ≥ 30
- Minor: 29 ≥ SS ≥ 0

**Probability Score (PS):**
- Very likely: PS = 100 (likely or certain to occur)
- Likely: 99 ≥ PS ≥ 70 (can occur, but not probable)
- Unlikely: 69 ≥ PS ≥ 30 (not likely to occur)
- Remote: 29 ≥ PS ≥ 0 (occurrence essentially zero)

**Formula:** PS + SS = RS (Risk Score)

**Risk Score Categories (Table 2):**
| Risk Score | Category |
|---|---|
| ≥ 160 | High |
| 120–159 | Medium |
| 90–119 | Low |
| 0–89 | Negligible |

> Example: SS = 95 (very severe) + PS = 80 (likely range) = RS 175 → **High**

### 6.5 Hybrid Tool

#### 6.5.1 General

Hybrid tools combine two or more approaches (e.g. a numerical scoring system with a risk matrix, or a risk graph with a matrix for one of the parameters). A certain amount of quantification can be incorporated into qualitative approaches by giving frequency ranges to probabilities or exposures.

#### 6.5.2 Example Hybrid Tool

This tool quantifies the qualitative parameters. It is a hybrid of numerical scoring and a risk matrix, used in conjunction with the following guidance:

**Risk estimation phases:**
- **Pre-risk estimation** — concept phase only; no detail drawings; used to decide major systems (e.g. mechanical drive line, servo drives, hot air/ultra sonic sealing, movable guard, light barrier)
- **Intermediate risk estimation** — performed during machine development; two sets of hazards dealt with: those carried over from pre-risk phase (now implemented) and new hazards arising during development; follows design reviews
- **Follow-up risk estimation** — done on implemented protective/risk reduction measures; no new hazards should appear; if a new hazard is identified, it must also be estimated and evaluated

**Scoring parameters:**

*Severity (Se):*
1. Scratches/bruises cured by first aid
2. More severe scratches/bruises/stabbing requiring medical attention
3. Normally irreversible injury; slightly difficult to continue work after healing
4. Irreversible injury making it very difficult to continue work after healing

*Frequency (Fr):*
2. Interval between exposure > 1 year
3. Interval > 2 weeks but ≤ 1 year
4. Interval > 1 day but ≤ 2 weeks
5. Interval > 1 hour but ≤ 1 day
(Duration < 10 min may decrease values by one level; interval ≤ 1 hour = 5, not decreasable)

*Probability (Pr):*
5. Very high
4. High
3. Possible
2. Rarely
1. Negligible

*Avoidance (Av):*
3. Impossible
2. Possible

**Risk Classes:** C = Fr + Pr + Av

| Class | Se | Risk Zones |
|---|---|---|
| 5–7 | 1–4 | Grey area (Medium risk) |
| 8–10 | 3–4 | Black area (High risk) |
| 11–15 | 4 | Black area (High risk) |

## 7. Risk Evaluation

**Objectives of risk evaluation:**
- Decide which, if any, hazardous situations require further risk reduction
- Determine whether required risk reduction has been achieved without introducing further hazards or increasing other risks

When protective/risk reduction measures are applied, a new iteration of risk assessment must verify their effectiveness.

**Key considerations:**
- Hazardous situations with an extremely low (trivial) risk may be excluded from further consideration
- High-risk hazardous situations warrant a more detailed risk estimation
- Requirements of relevant standards (e.g. IEC 60204-1) must be considered
- Risk evaluation is only one input to the decision to stop iterating — also consider regulations, laws, work organisation, technical limits, and economics
- Do not overlook simple, effective measures for low risks due to exclusive focus on highest risks

---

## 8. Risk Reduction

Risk reduction is achieved by implementing protective/risk reduction measures in order of preference.

### 8.2 Inherently Safe Design

#### 8.2.1 Elimination of Hazards by Design

First priority — most effective because it removes the source of harm.

Examples:
- Substitution of hazardous materials and substances
- Modification of physical features (elimination of sharp edges, shear points)
- Elimination of repetitive activities and harmful postures

#### 8.2.2 Risk Reduction by Design

Where hazards cannot be eliminated, apply inherently safe design measures.

**Reducing severity of harm:**
- Reducing energy (lower force, lower hydraulic/pneumatic pressure, reduced working height, reduced speed)
- Using technical safety equipment to prevent/reduce a hazard (e.g. ventilation to prevent explosive/hazardous vapours)

**Reducing exposure to the hazard:**
- Reducing the need to be in a hazardous situation (mechanisation/automation of loading/unloading or feeding/removal operations)
- Relocating the source(s) of harm

**Reducing occurrence of hazardous events:**
- Improving reliability of components (mechanical, electrical/electronic, hydraulic/pneumatic, software)
- Applying safe design measures to safety-related control system parts (basic safety principles, well-tried safety principles, redundancy, monitoring)

> Where a protective measure is implemented via a control system safety function, implement in accordance with ISO 13849-1 and ISO 13849-2.

### 8.3 Safeguarding

Applied when hazards cannot be eliminated or adequately reduced by design. Use guards and protective devices to:
- Restrict exposure to hazards
- Lower the probability of the hazardous event
- Improve the possibility of avoiding or limiting harm

**Safeguards with greatest impact on exposure (a and b):**
- Fixed guards, fencing, or enclosures preventing access to hazard zones
- Interlocking guards preventing access to hazardous areas (with or without guard locking, interlock keys)

**Safeguards with greatest impact on occurrence of hazardous event (c to e):**
- Sensitive protective equipment (SPE) for person detection in the hazard zone (e.g. light curtains, pressure-sensitive mats)
- Devices associated with safety-related control functions (enabling device, limited movement control, hold-to-run control)
- Limiting devices (overloading/moment limiting devices, pressure/temperature/over-speed switches, emission monitoring devices)

### 8.4 Complementary Protective/Risk Reduction Measures

Applied as required by intended use and reasonably foreseeable misuse.

Examples with greatest effect on ability to avoid or limit harm:
- Emergency stop (ISO 12100:2010, 6.3.5.2)
- Measures for escape and rescue of trapped persons (6.3.5.3)
- Measures for safe access to machinery (6.3.5.6)
- Provisions for easy and safe handling of machines and heavy component parts (6.3.5.5)

Examples with greatest effect on exposure:
- Isolation and energy dissipation measures (isolation valves/switches, locking devices, mechanical blocks)

### 8.5 Information for Use

#### 8.5.1 General

Provides guidance for correct and safe use. Informs users about residual risks after risk reduction by design and safeguarding.

Information for use impacts mainly the ability to avoid harm and persons' ability to understand and respond appropriately.

Can include information about required training and use of PPE.

#### 8.5.2 Consideration of Training

The instruction handbook can contain any training necessary to ensure persons know how to correctly use the machinery and apply any protective measures. Training is most important when the effectiveness of protective measures depends on human behaviour.

Regular review and checking of training effectiveness may be necessary. Training mainly impacts:
- Ability to avoid harm
- Exposure reduction
- Probability of occurrence of a hazardous event

#### 8.5.3 Consideration of Personal Protective Equipment

Information for use can provide guidance on any PPE required to protect against residual risk hazards.

PPE reliability and maintenance is important for long-term efficiency. PPE impacts exposure and the ability to avoid or limit harm.

### 8.6 Standard Operating Procedures

The supplier should give details of any SOPs the user should adopt in the instruction handbook. These may include:

- Work planning and organization
- Clarification/harmonisation of tasks, authority, responsibilities
- Supervision
- Lock-out procedures
- Safe operating methods and procedures

> When residual risk reduction is provided by organisational measures, ensure, as far as possible, that they are followed and cannot be circumvented.

---

## 9. Risk Assessment Iteration

Once protective/risk reduction measures are incorporated, all stages of risk assessment should be repeated to check whether:

- There are any changes to the limits of the machinery
- Any new hazards or hazardous situations have been introduced
- Risks from existing hazardous situations have been increased
- The protective/risk reduction measures reduce risk sufficiently
- Any additional protective/risk reduction measures are required
- Risk reduction objectives have been achieved

Iteration must account for: reliability, ease of use, possibility of defeating or circumventing protective measures, and the ability to maintain them in accordance with ISO 12100:2010, 5.5.3.5, 5.5.3.6, and 5.5.3.7.

---

## 10. Documentation of Risk Assessment

Written records must be made and retained. These are not the same as the information for use provided to the user, but can be a useful reference when writing it.

Documentation must record:
- The method(s) and tool(s) used to conduct the assessment
- Results of the assessment (ISO 12100:2010, Clause 7)
- Figures, photographs, diagrams, drawings of machinery including hazard zones, hazards, and applied protective/risk reduction measures

When documenting protective/risk reduction measures, include a description of the measures needed to ensure they remain effective (e.g. maintenance, periodic user inspection).

This example demonstrates application of risk assessment and risk reduction during the design of a single-spindle vertical moulding machine, in accordance with ISO 12100. It is non-exhaustive and not intended as a model to follow. It represents one possible way of applying the ISO 12100 principles.

### A.2 Information for the Risk Assessment

#### A.2.1 Initial Specifications of the Machine

**Basic specifications:**
- Stationary single-spindle vertical moulding machine
- Indoor use
- One operator
- Hand-fed
- Electrically supplied

**Work to be performed:**
Modifying the profile of square or rectangular cross-section wooden pieces and analogous materials (cork, chip board, fibre board, hard plastic) by moulding, rebating, and grooving.

**Work types:**
- **Straight work** — one face on the table, one on the fence; workpiece starts at one end and continues through to the other
- **Stopped straight work** — machining only part of the workpiece length
- **Curved work** — one side in contact with the table (or, using a jig, the jig on the table) and the other in contact with the vertical reference of a steady or ball ring guide

**Exclusions:**
- Not for tenoning
- Only wood products clear of foreign objects (e.g. nails)
- Not for metallic materials
- Only standard cutting tools available in the market

**Machine concept (Figure A.1):**
- Cutter tool mounted on a vertical spindle; turns in one direction only
- Spindle raised and lowered via hand-wheel (spindle unit); four speeds via electric motor and pulleys
- Spindle unit and driver unit anchored to cast iron table resting on steel cabinet
- Workpiece guidance via appropriate guides
- Spindle speed manually selected by repositioning a transmission belt

**Preliminary design specifications:**
- Electrical supply: 50 Hz / 3/400 V/PE
- Power supply earthing: TT system
- Motor power: 4 kW
- Table dimensions: 1250 mm × 700 mm
- Spindle diameter: 50 mm; useful length: 180 mm; vertical adjustment range: 200 mm
- Spindle speeds: 3,000 / 4,500 / 6,000 / 7,500 min⁻¹
- Tool diameter: 120 mm to 220 mm maximum

#### A.2.2 Experience of Use

Most reported accidents involve contact with the tool — contact during kickback and tool snatching, mainly during straight work. Common causes:

- Not using a guard
- Using an inappropriate guard
- Not using guide rails (false fences), pressure pads, jigs, templates, or end stops

Less frequent: impacts from kickback of the workpiece, ejection of chips or parts of the tool or machine, fire from wood dust/chips.

Health damage can result from:
- Noise generated at the milling process
- Wood dust
- Fumes or substances released while milling impregnated or treated (preserved) wood

#### A.2.3 Regulations, Normative References and Technical Sheets

Standards considered: ISO 12100, ISO 13849-1, ISO 13849-2, ISO 13852, ISO 14118, ISO 14119, ISO 14120, IEC 60204-1, EN 614-1 (ergonomics), ISO/TR 11688-1 (acoustics).

Technical sheets from national health and safety institutions (INRS, HSE, BG, OSHA) were consulted.

### A.3 Determination of the Limits of the Machinery

#### A.3.1 Life Cycle Phases

Significant phases:
- **Transport** — internal transport, removing, etc.
- **Assembly, installation and commissioning** — removing transport parts, connecting power, checking correct rotation, checking controls
- **Setting** — changing tools, mounting and adjusting guides, changing spindle speed, trials
- **Operation** — hand-fed milling
- **Cleaning, maintenance** — greasing rotating/transmission elements, changing belts, cleaning internal parts

#### A.3.2 Use Limits

**A.3.2.1 Intended Use:**
- Modifying profile of square/rectangular wooden pieces and analogous materials
- Professional use only
- Operator: knowledge and experience with this machine type; no limitations in physical abilities of upper limbs; no uncorrected visual impairment

**A.3.2.2 Reasonably Foreseeable Misuse:**

- Use by untrained operators

**A.3.2.3 Space Limits:**
- Indoor industrial environment
- Flat area of at least 3,000 mm × 3,000 mm, free of obstacles
- Connected to dust extraction system

- Brake: verify stopping time < 10 s, every day

### A.5 Risk Estimation, Evaluation and Risk Reduction

#### A.5.1 Risk Estimation Method

The **risk graph method** (6.3) was used.

For health/ergonomic and fire hazards, the risk graph is not fully appropriate. Assumptions applied:
- **Health and ergonomic hazards:** Only severity and exposure considered; for probability of occurrence of a hazardous event and possibility of avoidance, the most conservative value was assumed.
- **Fire hazards:** Parameters of severity, exposure, and probability can be associated with size/strength of the potential fire. Where avoidance is possible, it is difficult to make a real estimation, so the most conservative value was assumed.

**Risk Graph Parameters used (A.5.2 abbreviations):**

| Parameter | Classes |
|---|---|
| **S** Severity | S1 = slight, S2 = serious |
| **F** Exposure | F1 = seldom, F2 = frequent |
| **O** Probability of hazardous event | O1 = very low, O2 = medium, O3 = high |
| **A** Possibility of avoidance | A1 = possible, A2 = impossible |
| **RI** Risk index | 1 (min) to 6 (max) |
